PFAS are industrial “forever chemicals” from the military-grade firefighting foam sprayed at Miami International Airport for decades. How is this score calculated? +
Four benchmarks, weighted for the protection of children and log-scaled so early exceedance registers early:
  • Total PFAS burden — 30%. Every detected compound, short-chain and long.
  • Legal — 25%. Regulated PFOS / PFOA vs the 4 ppt federal limit.
  • Health — 25%. The same vs the EPA’s 2022 advisories (0.02 PFOS, 0.004 PFOA) — goal: zero.
  • Persistence — 20%. Long-chain compounds that build up in the body.
100 means the health goal is met, not merely the legal one. Read from your utility’s own EPA UCMR5 reporting (2023–25). The arithmetic is shown under the card — never a black box.
Short-chain vs long-chain — what to look for +
Long-chain PFAS (PFOS, PFOA) are the regulated ones — they bind to blood proteins and take about 5 years to fall by half. Short-chain PFAS (6:2 FTS, PFPeA, PFHxA) are present in the largest amounts in Miami — up to 190 ppt of firefighting-foam compound at the airport-side plant — and have no legal limit at all. A water report can be legally compliant while its largest contaminant is unregulated.
Bioaccumulation & children +
Persistence: the carbon–fluorine bond does not break down — not in the environment, not in your body — which is why PFAS are called “forever chemicals.” Bioaccumulation: because intake outpaces the body’s slow clearance, the amount in your blood climbs with every year you drink the same water, settling at roughly 100× the concentration in the water itself. Children are most exposed: they drink far more water per pound, and have a lifetime of accumulation still ahead.

WHAT IS PFASWHY IT BUILDS UP

What is PFAS — and why it builds up

PFAS (say “PEE-fass”) are a family of thousands of man-made chemicals that make things non-stick, waterproof and stain-proof — and they are the active ingredient in the firefighting foam sprayed at airports and fire-training sites for 50+ years. They are built on the carbon–fluorine bond, the strongest in chemistry, so they do not break down — not in soil, not in water, not in the human body. That is why they are called “forever chemicals.” They are persistent (they don’t break down), bioaccumulative (they build up in blood and tissue), and biomagnifying (they concentrate up the food chain) — which is why total load matters, not any one compound.

Once you drink them, they bind to the proteins in your blood and leave only slowly: the body takes years to clear even half of a single dose — about 5 years for PFOS, up to 8 for PFHxS. In a home drinking the water every day, new PFAS arrive faster than old PFAS leave, so the amount in the body climbs year after year; peer-reviewed pharmacokinetics indicate the level in the blood settles at roughly 100× the level in the water. That is why the EPA’s health goal is zero — it has found no dose that is safe.

And it is not only what you drink. Because PFAS leave the body so slowly, what matters is your cumulative load — the sum of every water contact, every day, for years: cooking, coffee and ice, washing produce, and the warm-water, long-contact routes of bathing, showering and swimming, where emerging evidence shows PFAS can cross the skin. Small daily exposures across many ordinary moments add up to a body burden that keeps climbing.

Because it accumulates, PFAS matters most for the smallest and still-developing bodies: it crosses the placenta and passes into breast milk, and children take in far more water per pound of body weight than adults. Pregnant and nursing mothers, infants, and young children carry the most risk from the same water.

Long-term exposure is linked, in the EPA’s and independent scientific record, to kidney and testicular cancer, thyroid disease, ulcerative colitis, high cholesterol, and pregnancy-induced hypertension — the six “probable links” established by the 69,000-person C8 Health Project. Separately, peer-reviewed studies tie childhood exposure to a weakened vaccine response, to effects on brain and neurological development, and to lower birth weight. In 2023 the WHO’s cancer agency classified PFOA as a Group 1 human carcinogen, its highest category.

In Miami-Dade specifically, thyroid cancer is the 2nd-highest of Florida’s 67 counties and runs more than double the U.S. rate in the hardest-hit neighborhoods beside the airport — the same corridor where PFAS is highest. This is an association, not proof of cause; the map’s Health section lays out the evidence and the honest caveats.

The good news: removing PFAS is a solved problem, not a theoretical one — proven methods remove up to 99.9% of it, including anion (ion) exchange, reverse osmosis, and atmospheric water generation. Reverse osmosis and ion exchange already run at municipal scale in neighboring Hialeah and North Miami Beach, and the same fix works at home \u2014 a certified anion-exchange stage into reverse osmosis, or an atmospheric water generator into reverse osmosis. A carbon or pitcher/fridge filter is a false comfort — it misses the short-chain PFAS and fades as it loads, so it is a stage, never the whole answer; and boiling only concentrates PFAS. Boiling does not; it only concentrates it.

Why is thyroid cancer elevated in Miami?

Of every chemical in Miami’s water, one has an address. PFAS disrupts the thyroid more directly than any other gland. And in Miami-Dade, thyroid cancer isn’t one hotspot you can explain away — it’s nearly the whole county, hottest right where the airport’s firefighting foam reaches the water.

Why the thyroid, specifically

In the blood, PFAS rides transthyretin — the protein that carries thyroid hormone — and blocks the iodine pump the gland needs to make it. The everyday version, hypothyroidism, isn’t tracked anywhere in Florida, so cancer is the only mappable tip.

Why it isn’t just “more screening”

If this were wealthy neighborhoods getting more scans, it would concentrate there. It doesn’t. Income and insurance explain only about 22% of the spread, thyroid tracks none of the other cancers, and working-class corridor cities sit at the very top.

What we do and don’t claim. This is an association, not proof of cause — a city-level pattern, not a verdict on any person. The contamination isn’t new — the “safe” line just fell toward it

Measured PFOS in Miami’s finished water (red) has sat in a narrow, high band for over a decade. The regulatory “safe” level (green) collapsed underneath it.

The federal government first measured PFOS in Miami’s drinking water in 2013 — 43 to 58 ppt across the county’s systems (EPA UCMR3). A decade later it is 31 to 38 (utility reports, 2023–25). The water did not get dramatically worse. What changed is the definition of “acceptable”: from no limit at all, to a 70 ppt advisory (2016), to a 4 ppt enforceable limit and a health goal of zero (2024). The line dropped roughly 18-fold — toward a contamination that was already there. (No finished-water PFOS was publicly reported for 2016–2022; UCMR3 ended in 2015 and UCMR5 did not begin until 2023.)

Two cities, two ways to get water

A viral map recently called New York’s water system “genius.” It is — and the comparison explains Miami exactly.

New York moves roughly a billion gallons a day to nearly 10 million people, almost entirely by gravity, from three protected watersheds up to 125 miles away — through tunnels like the 85-mile Delaware Aqueduct, the world’s longest. Because the land around the source is kept wild, the water arrives clean enough that the EPA permits it to run unfiltered. The design puts distance and protection between people and their water.

Miami-Dade’s design is the opposite. Its drinking water is pumped from the Biscayne Aquifer — an unconfined limestone formation whose top sits just 2 to 5 feet below the surface, so permeable that a spill at ground level reaches the water table quickly. Some of the wellfields sit right beside Miami International Airport, where firefighting foam was used for decades — and the treatment plants do not remove PFAS. There is little distance, and little protection, between the surface and the glass.

How we know it’s the airport and firefighting foam

This whole report is built from the government’s own records. Here is the chain — and the honest limits of it.
The chemical fingerprint

6:2 FTS is a marker of aqueous film-forming foam (AFFF) — the firefighting foam sprayed at airports and fire-training sites since the 1970s. It does not occur in nature, so where it turns up in drinking water, foam put it there. In the EPA’s own federal monitoring, 6:2 FTS leaves the airport-side Hialeah plant at 190 ppt — the highest reading in the county — exactly where Miami International and the County’s own fire-training academy have used AFFF for decades. The County’s own groundwater monitoring at that academy measured PFOS in the tens of thousands of ppt (groundwater at the source — not drinking water). The chemistry, the location, and the decades of foam use all line up: the source is AFFF.

The Miami Springs wells it took out of service had been feeding the tap at 124, 182 and 184 ppt. Its own director later put it at 70 of 95 wells with detectable PFAS. Its own official confirmed the treatment plants “do not remove PFAS,” and the utility says it manages which wells it runs “to maximize output from more pristine sources.” The delivered number therefore measures the blending, not the source.

PFAS in Miami is not only an airport story. The peer-reviewed record also attributes PFAS to wastewater, landfills and industry, and the physically separate South Dade system — 25 miles away, near Homestead’s own airfield — runs high too. AFFF at Miami International is a documented, dominant hotspot; it is not the only source. We would rather tell you that than have you hear it from the other side. It is even in the rain, at single-digit levels — a real but minor route we publish precisely so no one can wave the tap numbers away as “just background.”

When another utility found PFAS, it warned people

A compliant water system in Wisconsin shows the honest alternative — and a state health department recommends exactly the fix.

In a formal Drinking Water Advisory, Walworth Waterworks in Wisconsin reported PFAS in one of its wells above health-based standards. Its state health department’s response was to tell residents to reduce their exposure: the Wisconsin Department of Health Services “recommends people limit their intake of PFAS compounds,” and advised limiting consumption of the utility’s water and using a certified anion-exchange → reverse-osmosis filter (NSF/ANSI 58). “Boiling water does not remove PFAS.”

The part that matters

The same advisory states the utility “is not in violation of any federal or state drinking water standards or regulations.” Fully compliant — and it still warned its customers, in writing, to drink less of the water and to filter it.

Miami-Dade’s finished water runs about 8× the federal PFOS limit. It, too, is compliant — utilities are not required to meet the limit until April 2029. Its 2025 report calls the water “high-quality” and communicates its “excellence.” Two utilities, the same federal law, opposite messages. You decide which one is protecting your family.

One honest note: the Wisconsin advisory reflects the cautious agency position that skin contact is low-risk. Newer peer-reviewed work (Birmingham, 2024) finds many PFAS do absorb through skin — the science is still moving. Both agree drinking is the dominant route, and both point to the same certified filters.

The next chapter of the oldest public-health work

In 1842, the lawyer and reformer Edwin Chadwick showed — in his landmark 1842 report — that clean water is the foundation of a healthy population, and helped launch the sanitary revolution in Britain: engineered sewers, filtered municipal water, and the Public Health Act of 1848.

The payoff was the largest in the history of medicine. Readers of the British Medical Journal (BMJ) later voted that “sanitary revolution” — clean water and sewage disposal — the greatest medical advance since 1840, ahead of antibiotics and vaccines. Clean water alone drove roughly half of the U.S. mortality decline in the early 20th century and helped lift life expectancy from 47 to 63 years (Cutler & Miller, 2005).

That work removed what people could see and smell — the pathogens behind cholera and typhoid. In Miami-Dade, the water is microbially clear; that first battle was largely won. What it carries now is what the Victorians never faced: invisible, man-made industrial chemicals that do not break down and slowly accumulate. This is the next chapter of the same story — and the same principle holds: it is wiser, and historically cheaper, to protect the water at the source than to treat the consequences downstream.

Sources: Chadwick, 1842 (UK Parliament, Living Heritage) · Cutler & Miller, “The Role of Public Health Improvements in Health Advances,” Demography (2005) · BMJ readers’ poll (2007).

Persistence & how it builds up
PFAS are built on the carbon–fluorine bond, among the strongest in organic chemistry (~485 kJ/mol) — it resists water, oxygen, sunlight, microbes and human metabolism, so they never break down.
General chemistry; Rigano Water Institute whitepaper
PFAS leave the body slowly: it takes years to clear even half a dose — PFOA ~2.7–3.5 yr, PFOS ~4.8–5.4 yr, PFHxS ~5–8 yr.

Regulatory standards & the zero goal
First federally enforceable limit for PFOA and PFOS: 4 parts per trillion, finalized April 10, 2024.
U.S. EPA, PFAS National Primary Drinking Water Regulation (2024)
The health-based goal (MCLG) for PFOA and PFOS is zero — no exposure has been shown to be without risk. The 4 ppt limit is a feasibility compromise, not a safety threshold.
U.S. EPA NPDWR (2024)
EPA’s 2022 lifetime health advisories put the ‘no appreciable risk’ level at 0.004 ppt (PFOA) and 0.02 ppt (PFOS).
U.S. EPA, Lifetime Health Advisories, June 21, 2022
The 4 ppt limits are in force, but utilities have until April 2029 to comply. In May 2026 the EPA proposed extending that to 2031; that proposal is not final. Either way households drink the water in the interim.
U.S. EPA, 89 Fed. Reg. 32,532 (Apr. 26, 2024); EPA proposed compliance-extension rule, May 18, 2026 — not final
Thyroid
PFOS disrupts the thyroid: it knocks thyroid hormone (T4) off its carrier protein (transthyretin) and blocks the iodine pump (sodium-iodide symporter).
Weiss 2009; Ren 2016 (mechanistic)
Each doubling of blood PFOS raised the odds of thyroid cancer ~56% (OR 1.56), rising to 2.67 when measured a year before diagnosis.
van Gerwen et al. 2023, eBioMedicine 97:104831
Across Florida census tracts, living near a PFAS source independently predicts higher papillary thyroid cancer; Miami-Dade is among the most PFAS-dense counties.
Wild et al. 2025, IJERPH 22:1290 (PMID 40869875)
Miami-Dade’s thyroid-cancer rate (~18/100k) is ~40% above the U.S. and the 2nd-highest of Florida’s 67 counties, rising while the national rate falls.
NCI State Cancer Profiles (age-adjusted)
Florida’s primary young-adult thyroid cluster spans Miami-Dade + Broward + Palm Beach (RR 1.26, p<0.001).
Amin & Burns 2014, BioMed Research Intl (PMID 24868551) ⚑ primary citation / inference pending verification
Kidney & other cancers
The C8 Science Panel (69,000 people) found six probable links to PFOA: kidney cancer, testicular cancer, ulcerative colitis, thyroid disease, pregnancy-induced hypertension, and high cholesterol.
